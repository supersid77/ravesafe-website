Start here

Three things to do this week

Each one makes the next conversation easier.

Start early

Keep it right for their age and build it up over time. If they can hear it online or at school, they can hear it safely from you first.

Stay calm and factual

Ask what they already know, set clear boundaries and repeat the message: "I'm here to keep you safe." Skip the how-to detail.

Agree a safety plan

A code word or emoji, meeting points, transport home and a "get help early" rule. It cuts panic and makes them more likely to be honest when it matters.

Pause and remember this

Honesty saves time. Time saves lives.

If you only remember one thing, remember this. If something goes wrong, the most useful thing they can do is tell the truth, fast.

If they ever call you because a mate is unwell, tell them to stay with the person and get staff or medics straight away. If someone is unconscious, having a seizure or not breathing normally, call 999.

Common questions

The questions most parents ask

Five that come up again and again. Tap one to open it.

What should I say if my child asks, "Have you ever done drugs?"
  • Decide how you'll answer before it comes up.
  • Keep it brief if you share anything.
  • Bring it back to them: "What made you ask?"
How can they handle peer pressure at parties?
  • Give them simple lines: "No thanks." "Not tonight." "I'm driving." "I've got training tomorrow."
  • Give them an out they can blame on you: "My parents are picking me up. No questions."
  • Agree a code word or emoji that means "come and get me now".
What if they say "everyone's doing it"?
  • Stay neutral: "It can feel like that, but not everyone does."
  • Ask where it's coming from. Friends, social media, school.
  • Keep it about their choices and their safety, not arguing over numbers.
How do I talk about vaping, nicotine and cannabis when it feels "normal" now?
  • Make it a health and safety chat, not a moral one.
  • Talk about effects they'll recognise: sleep, anxiety, focus, motivation, money.
  • Calmly ask if it's around them and whether they've been offered it.
What if I find drugs in their room? Should I punish them?
  • Safety first. Stay calm. Don't storm in.
  • Pick a set time to talk, and aim for consequences that lower the risk rather than just punish.
  • If you suspect they're being pressured, dealing, or at serious risk, get advice quickly.

Keep it short. Stay calm. Keep it about safety.

A simple plan means less panic

Before they go out

The basics

  • Stay with friends. No solo missions.
  • Set a meeting point and a backup.
  • Charged phone plus a power bank.
  • Transport home sorted before they leave.

Welfare and medics

  • Find welfare and medical as soon as they arrive.
  • Getting help early is non-negotiable.
  • If anything's been taken, honesty helps medics act faster.

The mixing message

  • Mixing drugs, or drugs and alcohol, makes everything less predictable and more risky.
  • If something goes wrong, tell an adult or medic exactly what happened.

Don't diagnose. Look for patterns.

What changes should I look for?

One difficult week or bad mood rarely means drug use. Look for changes that keep happening over time, then start a calm, safety-first chat.

Mood and behaviour

  • More secretive, defensive or shut down than usual
  • Big mood swings that keep coming back
  • More risky decisions, arguments or acting on impulse

Body and routine

  • Sleep changes that stick around
  • Appetite or weight changes, looking run down
  • More illness, headaches and "I feel rough" days

Money and friends

  • Money going missing, or constant "can I borrow" requests
  • A new friendship group plus sudden secrecy
  • More lying about where they are or who they're with

What to do with this

  1. Name one thing

    Pick one thing you've noticed and say it calmly.

  2. Ask one open question

    Something they can't answer with yes or no.

  3. Offer a plan, not a lecture

    Talk about how to keep them safe, not about what they did.

You don't have to wait for a crisis

When to get backup

  • Their use is ongoing, their mental health is slipping, or the risk is rising
  • You suspect they're being pressured, exploited, or spending time somewhere unsafe
  • There's been a medical incident
  • Your gut is screaming at you. That counts. Get advice early.
